How the engine works — execution model, costs & metric definitions

No look-ahead. A strategy turns the candles into a target position (long or flat) at each bar's close, using only information available at that close. That position then earns the next bar's close-to-close return. Nothing decided at bar t can profit from bar t itself — that ordering is the whole defence against accidentally trading on the future.

Entries & exits. Strategy entries and exits fill at the signal bar's close. Risk exits (stop / target / trail / time) are checked intrabar on the bars that follow: a bar whose low touches your stop fills at the stop price, and a bar that opens beyond it (overnight gap) fills at the open — never at a price the market skipped. If a stop and a target both sit inside one bar the stop is assumed to hit first: intrabar ordering is unknowable from OHLC, so the engine takes the conservative reading. After a risk exit, the strategy's signal must go flat once before a new entry is allowed — otherwise a still-long signal would buy right back the next bar and the stop would have meant nothing.

Costs. Cost bps/side is charged on every entry and every exit (10 bps ≈ 0.2% round trip), a flat stand-in for brokerage + STT + slippage. The trade log shows both gross and cost-adjusted (net) returns; strategies that trade a lot live or die on this number. Every trade statistic below — win rate, profit factor, best and worst trade — is measured on the net figure, so a trade that gained less than it cost to put on counts as the loss it was.

Metrics. CAGR annualises total return. Max drawdown is the worst peak-to-trough fall of the equity curve; longest DD is the most bars spent below a prior peak. Sharpe is mean bar return over its volatility, annualised; Sortino penalises only downside volatility. Calmar = CAGR ÷ |max drawdown| — return per unit of worst pain. Profit factor = net wins ÷ net losses (>1.5 is respectable). Expectancy is the average net return per trade — what one more trade is "worth". Trades counts completed round trips only: a position still open at the end of the window has no exit price, so it appears in the log marked open but is left out of every statistic — otherwise an unrealised mark would show up as a win. Exposure is the share of bars spent in the market: a strategy matching buy & hold at 60% exposure earned it with less risk. All are compared against buy & hold on the same window, which is the number to beat.

Honesty caveats. One stock, one window, long-only, and parameters you can see — which means you can also curve-fit them. A result only starts to mean something when it survives other symbols, other windows and costs set higher than you hope for. Backtests are for rejecting bad ideas cheaply, not for proving good ones.

Field guide — every control explained thoroughly

Symbol. Any NSE/BSE instrument in the local index. Backtest the stocks you would actually trade: results do not transfer between a Nifty heavyweight and an illiquid smallcap, because slippage, gap behaviour and volatility differ completely.

Interval. The bar size the strategy sees. day means one decision per session at the close — the sane default for swing rules. Intraday intervals (5minute, 15minute…) run the same rules on faster bars: more signals, but each trade captures a smaller move while paying the same round-trip cost, so cost drag grows brutally as bars shrink. Kite also serves less intraday history, so long lookbacks may not fill.

Days. Calendar days of history to test — roughly 250 trading bars per 365 calendar days. 730 days ≈ 2 years is a minimum for daily strategies; 1800+ spans multiple regimes (a bull run, a correction, a chop phase), which is what actually stress-tests a rule. A strategy judged on 20 trades is being judged on noise.

Cost (bps/side). The friction charged on every entry and every exit, in basis points — 1 bp = 0.01%, so the default 10 bps takes 0.1% at entry and 0.1% at exit (≈0.2% per round trip). For Indian delivery trades the honest bill is: STT 0.1% on both legs, exchange + SEBI charges and stamp duty (~0.015%), GST on brokerage, plus slippage — the gap between the close your backtest assumes and the price you actually get, usually the biggest and least visible item. Discount brokerage may be ₹0, but the rest is not. Testing at 15–20 bps/side is a realistic-to-pessimistic band; if a strategy only works at 5 bps it does not work. High-frequency strategies (look at Trades and Avg bars held) are hit hardest — this single field kills more paper edges than any market move.

Strategy parameters. These appear next to the strategy and mean: fast / slow — lookbacks of the two moving averages being compared; the gap between them sets how decisive a trend must be before you act. length — the main lookback of the indicator (SMA length, RSI period, Bollinger/Keltner basis, CCI window…). Shorter = more sensitive, more trades, more noise. signal — the EMA length applied to the MACD line itself. mult — band width in standard deviations (Bollinger) or ATRs (Supertrend/Keltner): higher = wider bands, fewer but stronger signals. low / high — oscillator entry and exit thresholds (RSI, stochastic, CCI): enter below low, exit above high; the spread between them sets how much recovery you demand before leaving. entry / exit — separate breakout lookbacks (Donchian, volume breakout): close above the entry-bar high gets you in, close below the exit-bar low gets you out; a shorter exit protects profits faster. k / d — stochastic %K lookback and the SMA smoothing applied to it. trend — the long SMA regime filter (200 = the classic 200-day). adx / adx_len — the ADX trend-strength gate and its period; entries are allowed only when ADX ≥ the threshold. atr_len — the ATR period used for Keltner band width. lookback — the history window the Bollinger squeeze ranks today's bandwidth against. vol_mult / vol_len — volume confirmation: today's volume must be at least vol_mult × its vol_len-bar average for a breakout to count.

ATR — the unit behind the risk tools. The true range of a bar is the largest of: high−low, |high−previous close|, |low−previous close| — i.e. the bar's real travel including any overnight gap, which plain high−low misses. ATR is the Wilder-smoothed average of true range over N bars: "how far does this stock typically move in a bar, right now". Its power is that it is self-calibrating: a fixed 3% stop is meaninglessly wide for a placid largecap and suicidally tight for a volatile smallcap, but "3 ATRs" adapts to each stock and to each volatility regime automatically. The engine uses the same Wilder ATR your live alerts use.

Stop-loss %. A fixed floor: exit if price falls this % below your entry. Checked intrabar — a bar whose low touches it fills at the stop, a bar that gaps below it fills at the open (gaps do not respect your stop; the backtest shouldn't either). It caps single-trade disaster, and its cost is getting shaken out of trades that would have recovered — run the same window with and without it and read the trade log's Reason column to see which effect dominated.

Target %. A fixed take-profit above entry, same intrabar/gap fill rules. Targets convert open profit into realised profit and cap it in the same stroke — trend-following lives on rare huge winners, so a tight target can quietly delete the whole edge. Compare Best trade with and without.

ATR trail ×. The chandelier exit: stop = (highest high since entry) − mult × ATR. It only ever ratchets upward as the trade makes new highs — never loosens — so it gives a winner room to breathe early and locks in profit as the move matures. mult 2 = tight, 3 = classic, 4 = patient. This is the same maths as your live trail alerts, so a rule tested here can be executed there identically.

ATR period. How many bars the ATR averages. 22 ≈ one trading month, the standard chandelier setting; shorter makes the stop twitchier in volatility spikes, longer makes it steadier but slower to adapt.

Max bars held. A time stop: exit at the close once the trade is this many bars old, whatever the price. The logic: a mean-reversion trade's edge is concentrated in its first few bars — if the snap-back hasn't happened, the thesis is dead and the capital is hostage. Rarely useful for trend strategies, often excellent for reversion ones.

Interactions worth knowing. All enabled stops coexist: the effective stop is whichever sits highest (most protective). When a stop and a target fall inside the same bar the stop is assumed first — conservative by design. And after any risk exit, the strategy signal must go flat once before a new entry is allowed, so a still-long signal can't instantly buy back what your stop just sold.
